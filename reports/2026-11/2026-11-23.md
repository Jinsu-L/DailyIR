### 1. Structure-Exploiting Hierarchical Retrieval for Long Narrative Documents: A Precision-Oriented Alternative to Learned Abstraction Trees

- **LLM Score**: 0
- **Keyword Score**: 16
- **Authors**: Rohit Singh, Nathaniel Weems
- **URL**: <http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.29966v1>
- **Submitted**: 2026-11-23 03:30:50
- **Comment**: 10 pages
- **Topic Keywords**: retriever, dense retrieval, queries, ranking, retrieval, rank, search
- **Reason**: LLM scoring failed.

#### Abstract
> Retrieval-augmented generation over book-length documents must locate a small, coherent set of passages inside tens of thousands of tokens. Flat dense retrieval ignores document structure; RAPTOR instead builds a hierarchy by recursively clustering and summarizing with a language model. We study a complementary design that exploits the native structure of a document: a two-level retriever that (i) selects chapters with a hybrid BM25+dense match over chapter recaps, (ii) runs a chapter-filtered dense search, and (iii) expands top passages into contiguous, section-aware spans under a token budget, plus content-hash-gated delta ingestion to keep the index cheap to update. We evaluate on five public-domain books (113 chapter-localization queries) and on QuALITY and NovelQA (200K+ token novels). In-house, we find a seed-stable precision/ranking tradeoff: with section expansion off, hierarchical retrieval returns a higher fraction of chunks from the correct chapter (+3.3pp, Wilcoxon p=0.014); with expansion on, ranking improves (MRR, p=0.040). Accuracy differences are not significant. On QuALITY, hierarchical retrieval beats flat top-k (81.0% vs 70.4%, p=0.0019) and matches the full article (p=0.74). On NovelQA, hierarchical and flat retrieval are statistically tied (55.7% vs 56.8%, p=0.24); hierarchy helps aggregation questions while flat wins on localized facts. Against a faithful RAPTOR baseline we find a complementary tradeoff, not superiority. Delta ingestion reuses 94 to 99.7% of embeddings on incremental edits. We make no claim of semantic chapter-boundary inference and scope claims to English narrative prose.

#### Abstract
> Large language models (LLMs) answer many diet and health questions but can state unsupported claims and cite sources that do not support them. We present SHAKTI (Smart Health Advisor for Knowledge-based Tailored Interventions), a conversational assistant that answers only from retrieved scholarly papers and exact food-composition data, and evaluate it with retrieval, generation, grounding, efficiency and contextawareness metrics. Large-scale retrieval with agentic evidence selection and mechanical verification yields auditable, low-hallucination nutrition answers.

### 3. Weight Coding: Evolving Web-Scale Recommenders through Direct Weight Edits with LLM Agents

- **Topic Keywords**: retriever, dense retrieval, ranking, retrieval, recommend, rank, search

#### Abstract
> Web-scale recommender models evolve primarily through retraining or fine-tuning. We propose \emph{weight coding}, a methodology for model evolution through direct, executable weight edits. A large language model (LLM) agent inspects model structure and parameters, formulates hypotheses, and implements edits using evaluation feedback. The loop progresses from localized changes to targeted searches and composition, allowing edited components to adapt to one another. A cached engine enables rapid forward evaluation without backpropagation. On Pinterest's 147M-parameter Image Search two-tower retriever, weight coding improves held-out recall@10 of production model by up to 15.0 percentage points (+24\% relative) with few edits in about 1/4 GPU hours of a full post-training finetune, while a common launch only improves around 1\%. Our results establish weight coding as a practical and useful tool for model development and and behavioral control in a real web-scale production system, with potential to extend to both pre-training and post-training stages across the funnel.

#### Abstract
> Product search is a crucial component of large-scale e-commerce platforms, where first-stage retrieval should achieve high recall while ensuring efficient online deployment. Sparse retrieval remains attractive for this setting due to its interpretability and deployability, yet it suffers from severe vocabulary mismatch challenges, especially for short queries and product titles. Large language models (LLMs) offer strong semantic capabilities, but directly applying them to sparse retrieval in product search introduces new challenges: (i) semantic expansion may distort critical literal signals such as brand names and model identifiers; and (ii) learning meaningful sparse representations is difficult in the high-dimensional vocabulary space induced by LLMs.To address these challenges, we propose PROSPER, a framework that uses LLMs as sparse retrievers for product search while explicitly preserving lexical fidelity. ### 5. When Does Reasoning Help Entity Disambiguation? Small LLMs In-Domain and Under Shift

- **LLM Score**: 0
- **Keyword Score**: 13
- **Authors**: Samy Haffoudhi, Fabian M Suchanek, Nils Holzenberger
- **URL**: <http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.30334v1>
- **Submitted**: 2026-11-23 09:11:54
- **Topic Keywords**: ranking, rerank, listwise, pointwise, rank
- **Reason**: LLM scoring failed.

#### Abstract
> Entity disambiguation (mapping ambiguous mentions in text to entities in a knowledge base) is a foundational step in tasks such as knowledge graph construction, question answering, and information extraction. Recent work has applied LLMs to this problem, achieving stateof-the-art performance. Notably, reasoning LLMs have been shown to perform entity disambiguation in a true zero-shot fashion, generalizing across knowledge bases and domains. However, it is unclear whether this capability survives, or remains competitive, with smaller LLMs, where entity disambiguation has typically relied on reranking rather than reasoning. We ask when reasoning is worth its cost at small scale. We show that two distinct factors are at work: the reranker's uncertainty over the candidates locates where the paradigms disagree, while shift governs which is right when they do.

- **Reason**: LLM scoring failed.

#### Abstract
> Multimodal knowledge graphs (MMKGs) store entities with photos and names and link them by facts. MMKG methods predict links between known entity IDs, and image retrievers, from CLIP to MLLM embedders, return entities that look like a query photo or match its description. Neither answers a common question about a new photo: which graph entity is related to the one shown, such as the team of a pictured player or the city of a landmark. We call this knowledge graph relational image retrieval (KRIR): given a photo and a natural-language relation, retrieve the related MMKG entity, which rarely resembles the photo, whether the depicted entity is in the graph (known) or not (unseen). We build three benchmarks from YAGO, Wikidata and in-the-wild OVEN photos. The task hides two hops: recognising which graph entity is shown, and recalling what it is connected to. GraphSeer bridges them inside one multimodal LLM: it links the photo to the graph, reads the facts of the best match, and retrieves with the hidden state at which the model is about to say the answer. An unseen entity has no facts of its own, but every candidate answer does, so a listwise verifier reads the other end of the graph: it re-ranks the top candidates by their facts and photos in one forward pass, and the facts matter far more than the photos. With Qwen3.5-27B, GraphSeer improves MRR on known entities by 24.2-70.0% over an MLLM embedder fine-tuned on the same data with the same budget. When it also fuses and verifies the embedder's candidates, it improves MRR on unseen entities by 12.5-25.6%, and on known entities it beats KG-completion models given the ground-truth entity ID.

- **URL**: <http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.29532v1>

- **Topic Keywords**: query, queries, rag
- **Reason**: LLM scoring failed.

#### Abstract
> Test-time learning (TTL) enables large language models to adapt to unlabeled inputs during deployment. The model updates a lightweight adapter by minimizing the next-token prediction loss on the input it receives. When these updates persist across conversation turns, every user query serves both as the request answered in the current turn and as adaptation data for later turns. By choosing the queries used for adaptation, an attacker can influence parameter updates and weaken safety alignment through ordinary interactions with a TTL chat service. To demonstrate this risk, we introduce Progressive Test-Time Adaptation Poisoning (P-TAP), a query-only multi-turn attack that jailbreaks the model by poisoning its session adapter through the service's own updates. Among existing safeguards, screening incoming queries flags every attack stream before the final request, while a perplexity-based detector of unsafe adaptation flags none of the successful attacks.
