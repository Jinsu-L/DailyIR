### 1. Preference Is Not Position: Evaluating Reranking on NevIR in Fixed Hybrid Candidate Pools

- **LLM Score**: 0
- **Keyword Score**: 18

- **URL**: <http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.26260v1>
- **Submitted**: 2026-10-19 12:16:58

- **Topic Keywords**: query, ranking, rerank, pointwise, pairwise, retrieval, rank
- **Reason**: LLM scoring failed.

#### Abstract
> Near-identical passages can differ in a decisive condition, such as negation. A ranker may order such a pair correctly while placing the preferred passage far down the retrieved list. We examine this distinction using official NevIR pairs jointly covered by fixed candidate pools from dense, learned sparse, and BM25 retrieval. On 2,736 Test query directions, a pairwise-trained ranker improves strict per-query preference over fixed fusion (56.62% versus 52.30%) while reducing preferred-passage mean reciprocal rank (MRR) from 0.5764 to 0.3055. An exploratory fixed-model replay of a variant with background sampling and revised supervision yields MRR of 0.5859 and strict preference of 54.53%. Bounded pointwise reranking of the fusion top 20 improves both outcomes. Qwen3-14B-AWQ with fusion tie-breaking reaches 77.45% strict preference, 58.99% NevIR-style paired accuracy across 1,363 complete pairs, and preferred-passage MRR of 0.7509. A BGE cross-encoder reaches 69.77% strict preference and MRR of 0.7404. A post-hoc analysis finds that the branch where equal Qwen grades for the two designated passages trigger fusion tie-breaking accounts for 27% of evaluated directions and contains most remaining strict-preference failures. These findings support joint evaluation of pairwise preference, designated-passage position, and computational cost, while distinguishing model judgments from the combined ranking procedure.

#### Abstract

- **URL**: <http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.26105v1>
- **Submitted**: 2026-10-19 10:01:25
- **Topic Keywords**: retriever, cvr, retrieval, commerce, e-commerce, search
- **Reason**: LLM scoring failed.

#### Abstract
> Generative retrieval assigns each item a discrete semantic identifier (SID) and retrieves items by autoregressively generating SID sequences. In dynamic environments such as e-commerce, new items arrive continuously, requiring periodic updates to the SID-to-Item mapping and model to maintain retrieval timeliness. Each update, however, can invalidate search paths already learned by the retriever, and the quantitative relationship between mapping change and retrieval quality has not been systematically characterized. We study this problem with extensive controlled experiments over 67 days of large-scale e-commerce search logs and obtain three core findings. (1) SID semantic drift causes severe retrieval degradation that may be masked by large beam sizes: mapping updates reduce HitRate by 41.78-62.05% at beam size 1 but only 8.33-10.85% at beam size 1000, as redundant candidate generation conceals the true severity of drift. (2) The fraction of changed SIDs is strictly monotonically related to retrieval quality: as the remapped fraction increases from 0% to 72.75%, HitRate decreases monotonically, dropping up to 42.66% at beam size 1. Full remapping remains substantially worse than the preservation policy under warm-start training, indicating that short-budget continued training alone cannot repair topology disruption. (3) The SID topology-preserving principle makes even the simplest warm-start strategy highly effective: fixing old-item SIDs, inserting new-items, and warm-starting from the previous model improves old-item HitRate by 10% and new-item HitRate by 109%, validated across two non-overlapping update cycles. These findings distill into a concise deployment principle: insert new-items, freeze old-items; warm-start when possible, otherwise freeze; never change SIDs the model has already learned. A three-week online A/B test on TmallAPP shows UCTCVR +0.29% and GMV +1.16%.

- **Authors**: Yifan Wang, Yifei Du
- **URL**: <http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.26143v1>
- **Submitted**: 2026-10-19 10:27:04
- **Comment**: 44 pages, 10 figures, 15 tables. Code: https://github.com/ivanna10240823-rgb/flmnet
- **Topic Keywords**: rag, click, ctr, click-through rate, recommend
- **Reason**: LLM scoring failed.

#### Abstract
> Click-through rate (CTR) prediction underpins recommender systems and online advertising, where high-dimensional categorical features interact in combinatorially many ways. Several recent CTR models, such as FinalNet and QNN, report higher accuracy when a network also learns from its own averaged predictions, for example by running the same network under different dropout patterns and training each run toward the average. Why this self-distillation helps, and whether its random sampling is needed, remains unclear. This work shows that as the number of dropout patterns grows without bound, the objective reduces exactly to the label loss of the logit-averaged prediction plus a penalty on how much the dropout copies of the network disagree. When dropout does not affect how a network gates its features, this penalty can be approximated in closed form, and FLMNet is designed on this principle and trains without sampling. Networks trained from different seeds disagree in the same form, and distilling several of them into one network recovers part of their ensemble gain. On five benchmarks against seventeen baselines, the resulting models take the top two places in accuracy with a single network at inference, and the gains of the penalty and of distillation, where measurable, largely add up.

- **Topic Keywords**: query, queries, retrieval
- **Reason**: LLM scoring failed.

#### Abstract
> Long-running agents rely on memory of their past interactions for task state, such as what has been completed and which conditions still hold. Such state is usually a derived state. No record states it, and it must be computed by rules from multiple records of an evolving history. Existing memory benchmarks score only the final answer, with no reference for the intermediate states behind it. A wrong answer therefore cannot be traced to the state that was missed, and a state that silently became outdated goes undetected. We present MemDerive, a diagnostic benchmark for agent memory over evolving data. It specifies each scenario symbolically, derives exact gold answers with Datalog, records the gold derivation DAG and the supporting events of every query, and renders the history in natural language with fictional entities and distractors. Our evaluation of four memory systems reveals failures that final-answer benchmarks hide. Most failures are ungathered, as a system holds the missing intermediate state but does not retrieve it for the question that needs it. Memory systems also rarely propagate implicit changes, which no new record states. These results suggest three principles for memory design. Gather inputs along the derivation, store rules with their versions, and maintain derived states through recorded dependencies.
