### 1. Query Obfuscation with Embedding-Optimized Noise (EON): A Representational Blindspot in Agentic Retrieval

- **LLM Score**: 0
- **Keyword Score**: 16
- **Authors**: Ankit Gupta, Christoph Adami, Emily Dolson
- **URL**: <http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.31344v1>
- **Submitted**: 2026-11-23 17:53:49
- **Comment**: Preprint
- **Topic Keywords**: query, queries, ranking, relevance, retrieval, rank, search
- **Reason**: LLM scoring failed.

#### T.A.R.G.E.T. Summary (from Abstract)
- **Topic**: Representational failure in dense embedding retrieval pipelines and the BLINDSPOT vulnerability
- **Aim**: To expose how nonsemantic perturbations can preserve retrieval outcomes, introduce Embedding‑Optimized Noise (EON), and analyze its security implications.
- **Rationale**: Dense‑encoder systems are widely used for intent, tool, document, memory, and cross‑modal retrieval; if they can be fooled by noise, safety guardrails can be bypassed, posing a serious risk.
- **Ground**: Dual‑encoder retrieval architectures map queries to embeddings; similarity matching drives routing and search; prior work assumed semantic integrity of queries.
- **Experiment**: Constructed EONs solely from a query’s embedding, tested them across several dense families (e.g., BERT, CLIP, etc.), measured retrieval accuracy for intent routing, benchmark document search, episodic memory, and cross‑modal retrieval, and evaluated guard‑rail bypass success.
- **Takeaway**: EONs can maintain key retrieval functions while bypassing input guardrails, revealing the BLINDSPOT phenomenon where dual‑encoder systems are blind to nonsemantic artifacts yet retrieve real content, highlighting a critical security vulnerability.

#### Abstract
> In modern agentic and semantic retrieval systems, dense embedding models increasingly determine which intent, tool, document, memory, or image to retrieve for a query. We identify a representational failure mode in this pipeline: a meaningful query can be replaced by visibly nonsemantic content while preserving much of its retrieval outcome, even when the candidate bank is hidden from the optimizer. We introduce Embedding-Optimized Noise (EON), constructed using only the natural query representation, without access to candidate embeddings, relevance labels, rankings, retrieval feedback, or guardrail decisions. Despite this corpus-blind objective, EONs frequently preserve intent and tool routing, benchmark document search, episodic agent-memory retrieval, and cross-modal search across multiple dense embedding families. Crucially, this disconnect carries serious security implications: for malicious queries, an EON can reliably evade input guardrails that block the natural query, executing the intended safety-sensitive retrieval undetected. Through the geometry of the embedding space, we then characterize how query-level representation matching can reliably maintain subsequent retrieval outcomes over candidates unseen during optimization. Together, these findings expose a fundamental phenomenon across dual encoders that we call BLINDSPOT, where systems remain operationally blind to nonsemantic artifacts while reliably retrieving real, consequential content.

#### T.A.R.G.E.T. Summary (from Full Text)
- **Topic**: APT OR: Lightweight, Precision‑Oriented Retrieval System for Narrative Books
- **Aim**: Build a retrieval‑augmented generation system that localizes relevant passages in long narrative texts, keeps the index small and up‑to‑date, returns coherent, chapter‑consistent passages, raises evidence‑selection precision under a tight token budget, and provides a reproducible, fully open‑source baseline for long‑context QA on narrative prose.
- **Rationale**: Long narrative texts pose challenges for RAG: large context, sparse relevant passages, and high token costs. Leveraging the native chapter/section hierarchy allows coarse filtering, reduces retrieval cost, and improves precision. A lightweight, delta‑ingestion index keeps the system up‑datable without re‑embedding the entire book.
- **Ground**: APT OR is a three‑stage hybrid coarse‑to‑fine pipeline: (1) Chapter routing via BM25 + dense k‑NN over chapter recaps (1024‑dim embeddings) selecting top 3–7 chapters; (2) Passage retrieval within selected chapters (top 8 chunks per chapter) using OpenSearch kNN; (3) Optional section‑aware passage expansion up to a 1 500‑token budget, respecting section boundaries. The system uses a semantic chunker (agglomerative, cosine threshold 0.15–0.20) and delta ingestion keyed by SHA‑256 hashes to reuse embeddings. A flat k‑NN baseline (top 8 chunks from the whole novel) is used for comparison.
- **Experiment**: Datasets: five public‑domain books (113 chapter‑localization queries), QuALITY (~5 k‑token articles), NovelQA (~200 k‑token novels). Metrics: accuracy, MRR, Recall@3, precision. Results: In‑house books – hierarchical retrieval improves precision by +3.3 pp over flat kNN and yields +0.055 MRR when expansion is enabled; accuracy tied. QuALITY – hierarchical achieves 81.0 % accuracy, 10.6 pp above flat kNN and statistically indistinguishable from full‑article context. NovelQA – hierarchical and flat kNN tied (56.8 % vs 55.7 %). RAPTOR comparison shows no accuracy edge over flat retrieval but APT OR surpasses RAPTOR in ranking (MRR) and passage precision (p < 0.001).
- **Takeaway**: Using the book’s native chapter hierarchy in a lightweight, delta‑updatable retrieval pipeline yields higher precision and comparable or better ranking than flat k‑NN, while keeping token usage low. The system outperforms RAPTOR in precision and ranking, provides an open‑source baseline for long‑context QA on narrative prose, and demonstrates efficient maintenance through embedding reuse.

#### Abstract
> Retrieval-augmented generation over book-length documents must locate a small, coherent set of passages inside tens of thousands of tokens. Flat dense retrieval ignores document structure; RAPTOR instead builds a hierarchy by recursively clustering and summarizing with a language model. We study a complementary design that exploits the native structure of a document: a two-level retriever that (i) selects chapters with a hybrid BM25+dense match over chapter recaps, (ii) runs a chapter-filtered dense search, and (iii) expands top passages into contiguous, section-aware spans under a token budget, plus content-hash-gated delta ingestion to keep the index cheap to update. We evaluate on five public-domain books (113 chapter-localization queries) and on QuALITY and NovelQA (200K+ token novels). In-house, we find a seed-stable precision/ranking tradeoff: with section expansion off, hierarchical retrieval returns a higher fraction of chunks from the correct chapter (+3.3pp, Wilcoxon p=0.014); with expansion on, ranking improves (MRR, p=0.040). Accuracy differences are not significant. On QuALITY, hierarchical retrieval beats flat top-k (81.0% vs 70.4%, p=0.0019) and matches the full article (p=0.74). On NovelQA, hierarchical and flat retrieval are statistically tied (55.7% vs 56.8%, p=0.24); hierarchy helps aggregation questions while flat wins on localized facts. Against a faithful RAPTOR baseline we find a complementary tradeoff, not superiority. Delta ingestion reuses 94 to 99.7% of embeddings on incremental edits. We make no claim of semantic chapter-boundary inference and scope claims to English narrative prose.

### 4. When Does Reasoning Help Entity Disambiguation? Small LLMs In-Domain and Under Shift

- **URL**: <http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.30334v1>
- **Submitted**: 2026-11-23 09:11:54
- **Topic Keywords**: ranking, rerank, listwise, pointwise, rank
- **Reason**: LLM scoring failed.

#### Abstract
> Entity disambiguation (mapping ambiguous mentions in text to entities in a knowledge base) is a foundational step in tasks such as knowledge graph construction, question answering, and information extraction. Recent work has applied LLMs to this problem, achieving stateof-the-art performance. Notably, reasoning LLMs have been shown to perform entity disambiguation in a true zero-shot fashion, generalizing across knowledge bases and domains. However, it is unclear whether this capability survives, or remains competitive, with smaller LLMs, where entity disambiguation has typically relied on reranking rather than reasoning. We ask when reasoning is worth its cost at small scale. We show that two distinct factors are at work: the reranker's uncertainty over the candidates locates where the paradigms disagree, while shift governs which is right when they do.

#### Abstract
> Disk-resident approximate nearest-neighbor search (ANNS) scales vector search beyond DRAM, yet state-of-the-art systems remain I/O-bound, even with fast SSDs and sophisticated I/O execution.
