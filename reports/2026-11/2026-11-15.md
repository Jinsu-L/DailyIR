#### T.A.R.G.E.T. Summary (from Full Text)
- **Topic**: Sparse AutoEncoder for interpretable medical retrieval
- **Aim**: Transform dense LLM embeddings into k‑sparse, higher‑dimensional codes that preserve retrieval quality while enabling neuron‑level medical concept explanations and transfer across unseen corpora
- **Rationale**: Dense embeddings conflate multiple medical concepts, hindering interpretability; sparsity forces each neuron to capture a single concept, improving semantic clarity and reducing noise in similarity calculations
- **Ground**: Frozen LLM encoder (e.g., BGE‑large‑en‑v1.5) produces dense vectors; a linear encoder + ReLU + Top‑k selects k activations; decoder reconstructs the original vector; joint loss combines reconstruction error and InfoNCE contrastive loss to align clinically relevant query–document pairs
- **Experiment**: Trained on 244k clinical texts (CURE) with 2k queries; evaluated on five OOD corpora (MIRIAD, NFCorpus, R2MED Biology, Bioinformatics, Medical‑Sciences); metrics: NDCG@10; hyper‑parameters: latent dimension m>d, sparsity k≈64–256, λ∈[0,1]; results: in‑domain NDCG@10 ↑ by ~2–3%; OOD retention better than dense embeddings, with ~1–2% relative gain; sparsity level k≈64 balances over‑fitting and relevance
- **Takeaway**: Top‑k sparse autoencoders trained with joint reconstruction and contrastive objectives produce retrieval‑effective, neuron‑level interpretable codes that transfer across diverse medical datasets, offering a practical path to explainable medical information retrieval

#### Abstract
> Medical AI systems that answer users' queries rely on retrieving medical knowledge from large document collections. In this context, finetuning general models on medical datasets can improve retrieval relevance. Yet the dense embeddings underlying modern retrieval systems remain largely opaque, making it difficult to understand the mechanics behind their results. Moreover, adapting to a specific medical dataset risks losing transferability to new clinical corpora. In this work, we tackle these issues by applying Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs) to medical information retrieval, transforming dense text embeddings into semantically interpretable representations. To do so, our model combines a reconstruction objective with an InfoNCE contrastive loss that directly preserves query-document relevance. We evaluate three embedding models across six medical retrieval datasets, both in-domain and out-of-domain. Trained on a medical corpus, our sparse codes improve in-domain retrieval over the original embeddings, and lose less out-of-domain performance than their dense counterparts, especially when the target corpus is distant from the training one. Importantly, our experiments show that the method yields interpretable features whose semantic content can be inferred from the queries and documents that activate them most strongly. The interpretation of these features sheds light on why a particular document was retrieved, and transfers beyond the training distribution.

### 2. Interactive Program Induction with Language Model Priors

- **LLM Score**: 0
- **Keyword Score**: 8
- **Authors**: Robin Shing Moon Chan, Ryan Cotterell
- **URL**: <http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.18485v1>
- **Submitted**: 2026-11-13 17:59:55
- **Comment**: Preprint. 28 pages, 5 figures, 9 tables
- **Topic Keywords**: query, queries, acl
- **Reason**: LLM scoring failed.

#### Abstract
> Program induction refers to the process in which a learner aims to recover a program from a few given input-output examples. This paradigm broadens programming access by letting users demonstrate the rule-based behavior they want to apply to unseen data, instead of having to implement it themselves. However, recovering a program from finite input-output examples is fundamentally ambiguous, as a finite set of input-output examples is theoretically consistent with infinitely many programs whose output diverges on unseen inputs. We view this problem from a Bayesian perspective, where, given a prior $p^0$ over programs, identifying the target program means concentrating posterior probability mass on it by observing additional input-output examples. In the interactive program induction setting, the learner chooses maximally informative inputs and queries an oracle, such as the user, for their outputs. We derive a greedy query-selection algorithm and show that it terminates after $\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}γ\log 1/p^0(f^\star))$ informative oracle queries, and returns the target program $f^\star$ up to approximate equivalence when no other class outweighs it under the prior. The algorithm uses a language model as the prior under a PAC-style relaxation of program equivalence, where the query-selection objective is estimated using importance sampling. On the CodeARC benchmark, the algorithm improves accuracy over the LM only agent by up to 22 percentage points while observing up to 10.5 fewer input-output examples.

#### Abstract
> In retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), query rewriting serves as a core interface that aligns user intent with external knowledge bases, and its effectiveness directly determines overall system performance. However, generative retrieval faces three critical challenges in real-world deployment: noisy retrieval feedback, the difficulty of jointly optimizing discrete prompt templates and continuous decoding-control parameters, and the limited generalization of static rewriting strategies under semantically complex scenarios. To address these issues, we propose RAG-FGO, a dynamic optimization framework for generative retrieval strategies. RAG-FGO formulates query rewriting as a non-differentiable black-box optimization problem in a mixed discrete-continuous space, and represents prompt templates and decoding parameters as adaptively optimizable query-rewriting agents. The framework performs global search to enable semantic jumps and structural reorganization that escape local optima, while applying local perturbations for fine-grained alignment and precise refinement. In addition, RAG-FGO introduces an elite-preserving memory pool that accumulates high-quality strategies across iterations, thereby mitigating the impact of stochasticity and noisy feedback on optimization stability. Experimental results on a diverse suite of benchmarks demonstrate the effectiveness and robustness of the proposed method, supporting its applicability to complex semantic retrieval tasks.

#### Abstract
> Small open-weight language models can run crowd decision-making (CDM) on an institution's own infrastructure, which keeps the checkpoint, the inference settings and the opinions under its control. Their judgements vary across repeated queries; whether repeating and aggregating them, often through the 2-tuple linguistic representation, settles the decision rather than the labels is untested. - **LLM Score**: 0
- **Keyword Score**: 6

- **URL**: <http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.18030v1>
- **Submitted**: 2026-11-13 10:58:12
- **Topic Keywords**: ranking, rank, acl
- **Reason**: LLM scoring failed.

#### Abstract
> Industry now relies on LLMs to write a large share of production code. Although that code is mostly, but not always, correct, correctness alone does not determine whether it is fit for repeated and extensive use, which depends on what the code costs in time, memory and energy. Most code-generation benchmarks nonetheless evaluate correctness in isolation, and most performance-oriented benchmarks cover few languages and rarely measure energy consumption. To address this gap, we present PERFARENA, an open-source, reproducible arena that ranks LLMs by the performance of their generated code across multiple languages and competitions. PERFARENA executes the solutions of each LLM on a Unix-like host and reports correctness together with latency, peak memory, and energy consumption. Correctness oracles, performance oracles, profilers, and code-generation workflows are pluggable in PERFARENA, enabling the integration of new judging backends and measurement tools without changing the pipeline. We evaluate PERFARENA on two competitions: CLBGComp, 10 compute-intensive benchmarks from the Computer Language Benchmarks Game, and LeetCodeComp, 99 LeetCode problems whose accepted solutions differ measurably in latency. Currently, PERFARENA holds 9,455 correct and measured solutions from 13 state-of-the-art LLMs across 7 programming languages. Across both competitions, got-6-astra is the only LLM that no other LLM beats significantly in latency, energy consumption, or peak memory, solving 99.7% of LeetCodeComp and 100% of CLBGComp. All PERFARENA leaderboards and measured solutions are publicly available, providing a continuously updated reference that helps developers choose an LLM per language and metric, instead of relying only on correctness rates or pricing.

#### Abstract
> Efficient sequential recommendation has emerged as a promising paradigm for providing real-time personalized service. This "one-size-fits-all" approach leads to a fundamental Capacity-Complexity Mismatch, where computational resources are either wasted on simple instances or insufficient for complex ones. #### Abstract

#### Abstract
> Can you trust a finance agent you did not build? #### Abstract
> Before an LLM agent cancels a reservation or deletes a folder for a user, what stops it from acting on a false assumption: what its tools show, or what the user says? In this work, we study this gap with presupposition theory from linguistics: an agent may act only if each claim behind the act (e.g., one reservation fits the name) is seen to hold, answered by the user, or disclosed while the act can be undone. A simple bound shows that when lookups cannot tell one record from two, any agent acts without asking first as often with two records as with one. We then vary whether a search lists, counts, or omits a second match, and whether the user states a claim that a record refutes (e.g., "It is empty"). Under a role-only prompt, a count of a second record leaves most of 36 earlier agents acting on the one they see, whereas listing both stops most. When the user says a record such as a folder is empty, however, a count of its one item stops agents about as often as a listing, and stating the claim mostly cuts acting when it is false. The newest Claude and GPT models always open a counted second record, then never act. When we add a same-name, same-zip customer to a retail benchmark, a lookup returning that customer first led to wrong-account writes in about a quarter of conversations, and one that checks a street the user knows kept completion near the one-customer level. Safe writes therefore need lookups that list or check competing records, and evaluations that vary what tools show.

#### Abstract
> Governments are writing rules for artificial intelligence that decide what counts as an automated decision or a harm, often before the people they affect have a chance to question them. We introduce AI-Voyager, a public-interest system for human-AI civic reasoning, in which people and AI work together to understand, contest and shape the rules that govern AI. A resident can find their city's rules on AI in hiring or upload their country's AI strategy, read it as an affected worker would, open the passage behind any answer, and draft a revision. Its architecture turns 3,653 governance documents from city, state, federal and national governments into passages that keep their page and edition, and retrieves evidence through bounded agentic search that verifies each statement. On 360 new questions that require finding the relevant documents, it reached 83.0% macro recall, against 80.5% for the strongest baseline, 73.7% without iterative search and 99.1% on a benchmark with fixed documents. In a study with 24 participants new to two AI bills, most proposed changes asked how provisions would apply. Participants chose lenses for the kind of explanation they wanted, and citations signaled grounding but rarely led them to check the source. By binding AI assistance to the exact text of public documents, this work introduces a grammar of operations and a design space that inform how such systems are designed and evaluated, and how people take part in governing AI.
